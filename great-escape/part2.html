How They Actually Did It

A deeper look at the engineering, sand disposal, forged identities, escape night, the manhunt, and the details that make Stalag Luft III extraordinary.

A prison designed to stop tunnelling

Stalag Luft III was purpose-built to be difficult to escape from. It stood in a pine forest on loose, sandy soil, and the barracks were raised above the ground so guards could more easily notice digging. The prisoners answered that design challenge with an operation on the scale of a small engineering project.

Roger Bushell’s escape organization planned three major tunnels — Tom, Dick and Harry. The logic was simple: even if the Germans discovered one or two, they might not believe a third existed.

Did you know? The familiar phrase “Tom, Dick and Harry” did not come from the Great Escape. It had already been used for centuries as a way of referring to ordinary people or “just about anybody.” The prisoners borrowed those already-familiar names for their three tunnels — Tom, Dick and Harry.

How Harry was built

Go deep

Harry descended roughly ten metres before running horizontally toward the perimeter. That depth made detection harder, but every tool, timber and load of sand now had to move through a long vertical shaft.

Stop the sand collapsing

The same loose soil that German planners thought would defeat tunnelling also meant the passage needed constant shoring. Prisoners scavenged timber from the camp, including bed boards, to frame the narrow tunnel.

Build underground transport

Moving people and spoil through more than 100 metres of cramped tunnel by crawling alone would have been painfully slow. The prisoners built small trolleys and hauling arrangements so loads could be pulled along the passage.

Keep everyone breathing

At that depth, ventilation was an engineering problem of its own. The tunnel used improvised ducting and air-pumping equipment to push fresh air toward the digging face.

Hide the entrance

Harry’s entrance in Hut 104 was concealed beneath a stove. A dedicated security system of lookouts and “trappers” had to make the entrance disappear again whenever German guards approached.

The dirt problem

Excavating Harry produced an enormous volume of pale underground sand. It could not simply be dumped outside: the colour differed from surface soil and a fresh yellow pile would have advertised exactly what the prisoners were doing.

Some spoil was hidden in existing spaces. The camp theatre was particularly useful; tunnel sand could be deposited beneath its tiered seating, while rehearsals and performances created legitimate traffic and noise. The National Archives records former POW Jimmy James as one of the men who deposited sand beneath the theatre seats.

Other sand was dispersed gradually around the camp. The famous “penguin” method involved prisoners carrying concealed bags of sand inside their clothing and releasing small amounts while walking. The crucial trick was not making the sand invisible — it was spreading thousands of small quantities widely enough that no obvious spoil heap appeared.

Harry was not merely a tunnel. The prisoners had to hide the physical evidence created by digging a passage more than a football field long.

A whole fake world had to be created

Getting outside the wire was only the first few minutes of an escape. The men then needed to survive inside wartime Germany and occupied Europe. The escape organization produced civilian-looking clothing, forged identity and travel documents, maps, compasses, money, food and rehearsed cover stories.

A post-war report preserved by The National Archives says about 600 prisoners had worked on Harry and 200 were chosen for the breakout. The first thirty planned to use ordinary passenger trains; about forty more intended to travel part of the way on workers’ trains; most of the remainder expected to travel largely on foot.

Why only 200?

The tunnel could not empty an entire camp before daylight. Priority went partly to men who had made major contributions and to those thought most likely to survive outside — for example, men who spoke German or other European languages. A lottery also helped determine places in the escape order.

Escape night — March 24–25, 1944

They waited for darkness.
The breakout was timed for a moonless night. Two hundred selected prisoners had numbers determining their order through Harry.
Then came a nasty surprise.
The exit shaft had been miscalculated. Harry stopped short of the protective pine trees, so men emerged into open ground beyond the perimeter rather than safely inside the forest. Escapers had to coordinate each emergence with the movement of German sentries.
The system slowed.
Moving through the confined tunnel took longer than planned. A blackout connected with an air raid also caused disruption, particularly for men unfamiliar with the tunnel.
76 got out.
The National Archives records 76 men making it through Harry before the escape was discovered. The 77th was spotted and the breakout was stopped.

What did they do once outside?

There was no single route to freedom. Some men had convincing forged papers and attempted to blend into Germany’s railway system. Others planned to use workers’ trains or travel on foot, hoping eventually to reach neutral territory or friendly contacts.

Bushell himself was the fourth man through Harry. He travelled with French pilot Bernard Scheidhauer toward Sagan railway station, intending to travel west toward Alsace. They made it hundreds of kilometres before being arrested at Saarbrücken station on March 26.

The manhunt

The breakout provoked an enormous German security response. Railway stations, roads and borders were checked, dramatically reducing the odds of anyone remaining free for long. Of the 76 men who escaped, only three ultimately reached freedom; the other 73 were recaptured.

The three successful escapers were two Norwegians and one Dutch airman. Their success also shows how daunting the task was: getting through Harry was only the beginning.

The darkest part of the story

Hitler was enraged by the escape. Fifty of the recaptured Allied airmen were selected and murdered by the Gestapo. The killings were then disguised with false claims that prisoners had been shot while attempting further escapes.

Bushell and Scheidhauer were among those killed. After their arrest they were taken by the Gestapo, supposedly to be returned to Stalag Luft III. On March 29 their car stopped on the autobahn near Ramstein and both men were shot.

That is what gives the Great Escape its two very different legacies: an extraordinary feat of collective ingenuity, and the murder of 50 recaptured prisoners who should have remained protected POWs.

A few details that are easy to miss

The theatre was infrastructure

Entertainment was not just a morale boost. Performances could mask noise, provide plausible movement around the camp and even create space for hiding excavated sand.

Security was almost a full-time operation

Diggers underground depended on prisoners above ground spotting guards early enough to close traps, conceal tools and make rooms look normal.

Tom and Dick mattered too

The three-tunnel strategy spread the risk. Even the tunnels that did not ultimately carry the March 1944 breakout forced the prisoners to solve entrance concealment, spoil disposal and German countermeasures.

The movie compresses the reality

The famous 1963 film turned the story into a composite adventure. The real event was slower, colder, far more bureaucratic and logistical — and its ending was substantially darker.
